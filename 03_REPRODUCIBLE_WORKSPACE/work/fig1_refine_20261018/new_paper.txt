                                                Abstract
 Neural interfaces in biohybrid systems use stimulation and recording to interact with living networks.
 Under a limited recording budget, choosing where to record becomes part of predicting a specified
 stimulus response. Goal-oriented design ranks recording sets by their estimated prediction error. We
 test whether averaging local risk over a parameter prior improves the resulting response prediction
 or the accuracy of the risk estimate itself.
 We compared single-response and shared three-response objectives, each using a nominal-parameter
 or prior-averaged risk criterion, in simulated 12-unit networks with known connectivity and uncertain
 neuronal gains and biases. The target was the time-integrated difference between stimulated and
 control activity. All methods used the same feasible recording sites, measurement budget and
 maximum a posteriori estimator. The primary evaluation used 40 independent networks under broad,
 correctly specified parameter uncertainty, with three recorded neurons and stimulation and readout
 sites excluded. Each network was simulated with several parameter draws, noise draws and readouts;
 networks were the unit of analysis.
 Compared with the nominal criterion, prior averaging changed aggregate mean squared response
 error by -3.74% for the single-response objective (97.5% paired network-cluster confidence interval,
 -18.48% to 12.12%) and +2.69% for the three-response objective (-2.95% to 11.51%). Both confidence
 intervals included zero. The two criteria selected identical recording sets for 82 of 120 target-specific
 designs (three per network) and 26 of 40 shared designs. To evaluate risk estimates independently of
 recording selection, a separately specified study used 40 new networks with recordings, data and
 point predictions held fixed. The primary ensemble-minus-nominal score difference was -0.45565
 (95% confidence interval, -1.15857 to 0.02367), using log(v) + e2 /v, where v is the estimated mean
 squared error and e is the response prediction error. Nineteen of 40 networks favoured the ensemble
 forecast, one network supplied about 64% of the summed difference, and the nominal forecast risk
 was closer to the actual error in aggregate.
 In an exploratory extension to eight independent 100-unit rank-two networks, four of eight settings
 had 95% intervals that excluded zero, all favouring prior averaging; none excluded zero in the other
 direction, and one setting reversed in sign. Under the shared fitting procedure, prior averaging can
 affect response prediction only by changing which neurons are recorded.

  Key points

  • Decisions. The nominal and prior-averaged criteria chose identical recordings in 68% (82/120) of

  • Error and forecast. The two frozen primary comparisons and the primary forecast comparison

    connectivity and a correct observation model.



1 Introduction
A recording set should be judged by how well its data support the intended prediction. Biohybrid robots
translate optical inputs into motion through directly stimulated muscle or an intervening neuromuscular
interface [1, 2] (reviewed in [3]), and earlier neuromuscular bots coupled cultured neurons to muscle
tissue [4]; cultured neural networks also support decoding of responses to patterned electrical inputs
[5], and closed-loop use of cultured networks through a small electrode set has a longer history [6].
These systems motivate a task-specific view of neural measurements, although a neural response is not
itself a mechanical output. The inference may concern population activity or the response of a readout
to a planned stimulus, and under a limited recording budget these tasks value the same observation
differently. A recording-design evaluation must therefore connect the design criterion to the prediction
made from the selected data. This paper studies that connection in simulation only: the networks are
generic rate models, not a model of any biohybrid platform.
    Connectivity constrains neural dynamics but does not fix all neuronal properties. Different circuit
parameters can produce similar activity [7], and models in systems biology show a related pattern
in which many parameter combinations are weakly constrained by data although some predictions
remain well constrained [8]. Connectome-constrained modelling makes this ambiguity explicit: Beiran
and Litwin-Kumar study activity prediction with fixed connectivity, uncertain physiological parameters
and partial recordings, including the selection of recording sites [9]. Lappalainen and colleagues combine
anatomical structure, simplified dynamics and task optimisation to predict responses in the fly visual
system [10]. Both studies lay the foundation for asking how a restricted set of measurements constrains
a functional prediction. To our knowledge, neither audits whether a costlier, prior-averaged criterion
changes the selected sites or improves the prediction made from them. This paper tests that question.
    Validation must target the held-out response. Channel selection in brain-computer interfaces is
usually posed as choosing channels that maximise decoding performance on recorded data [11]; here the
choice is made before recording, from a model. Even high-density probes expose more sites than can
be read out at once [12], so a choice of recorded sites is still required; biohybrid preparations may be
more restricted. Sensor-selection studies in nonlinear networks distinguish structural observability from
the practical accuracy of state estimation [13], and budgeted sensor placement is commonly solved by
greedy selection with guarantees for suitable objectives [14]. These works treat the selection problem
well, but the guarantees concern the selection objective, not the error of a response predicted after fitting.
Agreement with the activity used for fitting does not by itself establish accuracy for an unobserved
perturbation. Sourmpis and colleagues use perturbation outcomes to assess neural models and the
contribution of biological constraints [15]. Here we fix the stimulation protocol and ask which calibration
recordings support its response prediction. The variable being designed is the recording set, rather than
the stimulation input, which has its own experimental-design literature [16, 17].
    Bayesian experimental design values observations by the expected utility of the resulting inference
[18, 19], and goal-oriented variants value them according to a quantity of interest [20]. A nominal-
parameter approximation is inexpensive; averaging local criteria over a parameter ensemble, as in related


                                                      2
designs, accounts for variation in sensitivities [21], while more complete nonlinear approaches address a
broader inference problem [22–25]. These designs refine the risk, but local sensitivity-based risk need
not track the error of a nonlinear response predicted after fitting. Two benefits must be distinguished:
forecasting the prediction error more accurately than the nominal risk, and changing the selected
observations enough to reduce that error. A revised risk estimate can leave the recording set unchanged.
Conversely, a design specialised for one known readout can outperform a shared design through task
specialisation alone. The study therefore compares criteria that differ in only one of these two choices.
Hypothesis: prior averaging improves response prediction or the forecast accuracy of the risk; both routes
require that the prior-averaged criterion differ from the nominal criterion in the recordings it selects or
in the forecast it reports. The key insight is that a risk criterion can change a prediction only through
the recordings it selects or through the forecast it reports, so each route can be audited separately.
    A controlled computational study evaluates both routes. Scalar and vector response objectives are
crossed with nominal and prior-ensemble local-risk criteria, using the same model information, feasible
recording pool, measurement budget and estimator. The evaluation has three layers: whether the criteria
choose different recordings, whether the response error of the fitted prediction differs, and whether the
prior-averaged risk forecast is more accurate than the nominal forecast for a fixed prediction. The first
frozen study tests actual response error in 40 independent networks. An independently specified follow-up
evaluates risk forecasts on 40 new networks while holding the data and point predictions fixed. Numerical
controls and a larger published model family examine implementation and modelling boundaries. The
main finding is that prior averaging often leaves the recording decision unchanged; the primary error and
forecast comparisons remain uncertain, and the extension identifies settings in which a benefit may exist.
    The paper makes three contributions. First, a factorial comparison, response objective crossed with
risk approximation under one information budget, that keeps task specialisation apart from uncertainty
handling. Second, a three-step audit of a recording criterion: how often it changes the recording decision,
whether the response error of the fitted prediction changes, and whether the prior-averaged risk forecast
is more accurate than the nominal forecast on a fixed prediction, scored with a rule that is consistent
for the mean squared error (Box 1). Third, an empirical finding for prior-averaged local risk: it usually
selects the same recordings and shows no clear gain in error or forecast accuracy in the primary condition,
with a conditional signal in a 100-unit family. The numerical controls, frozen protocol, source tables and
validation script support reproduction, and the exploratory signal is turned into a concrete confirmatory
hypothesis with planning caveats (Discussion, “Scaling and next studies”).

2 Results
2.1 A common information budget separates the prediction objective from
    the risk approximation
The simulated networks have known numerical connectivity J and uncertain neuronal gains and biases
(symbols in the Notation table, Methods). Calibration data were generated from a limited set of nodes;
the evaluation target was the time-integrated response to a separate fixed stimulus, relative to an
unstimulated control (Fig. 2). Three readouts were chosen using structural reachability before generating
physiological parameters or measurement noise. The scalar objective specialised its recording set for one
readout, whereas the vector objective used one set for the same three-response family. This construction
separates task specialisation from the comparison between nominal and ensemble risk. Throughout the
Results, “single-response” (scalar) and “shared” (three-response, vector) name the two objectives; the
abbreviations LS, ES, LV and EV, defined in Methods, combine objective and risk criterion.
    Box 1 and Fig. 1 summarise the audit. All methods fitted the selected observations with the same
Gaussian MAP estimator and predicted the response by nonlinear simulation. The principal candidate
pool excluded the stimulation site and all three readouts, preventing direct endpoint recording. All-
site recording and a larger budget were retained as additional conditions. Exact dataset reuse avoided
redundant fits when methods selected identical nodes; no observations from different scalar designs were
pooled. Box 1 (below) defines the three-step audit that structures the Results: Decision, Error and

2.3 A separately specified forecast test separates risk estimation from
    point-prediction accuracy
In the first study, an exploratory aggregate comparison suggested that ensemble risk could match actual
error better than nominal risk does, even when it did not improve the selected recordings. At the primary
nominal scalar recording set, actual MSE divided by nominal forecast risk was approximately 1.615; the
ratio using ensemble forecast risk was 1.103. Such a ratio does not establish forecast accuracy across
networks. The Forecast step of Box 1 therefore became a separate test, specified by us, using 40 new J
matrices, fixing the recording policy, selected data and fitted point predictions while comparing the two
risk forecasts.
    The primary ensemble-minus-nominal MSE-forecast score difference was −0.45565 (95% paired-
cluster interval, −1.15857 to 0.02367; Fig. 4). Nineteen network means favoured the ensemble forecast
and 21 favoured the nominal forecast. The shared point predictions had MSE 1.1781×10−4 , so no point-
prediction improvement can be attributed to changing the forecast alone. The interval does not establish
a stable forecast-score gain. The mean is also dominated by one network: its score difference of −11.67
accounts for about 64% of the summed differences, and the mean of the other 39 is −0.17. In aggregate,
the nominal forecast risk (1.19×10−4 ) was closer to the actual MSE (1.18×10−4 ) than the ensemble
forecast risk (1.92×10−4 ), so this study does not show that the ensemble forecast is better calibrated than
the nominal forecast. The exploratory aggregate hint from the first study (ensemble ratio 1.10 against
nominal 1.62) therefore did not replicate. No fit failures, fallbacks or forecast-floor clipping occurred.
    A secondary hidden-bias-shift comparison favoured the ensemble forecast by −1.3583 score units
(pointwise 95% interval, −2.2143 to −0.6345). Both mean risk forecasts nevertheless remained below
actual MSE in that condition. This result describes a relative forecast improvement in one secondary
setting; it does not show that the hidden shift was learned or that uncertainty was fully calibrated.

2.4 A larger model family suggests condition-dependent effects, several
    favouring prior averaging
An exploratory extension used eight independent 100-unit rank-two tanh networks from a published
mathematical family [9], with explicitly adapted calibration inputs and measurement noise. A coarse
first implementation failed convergence checks and was replaced by a refined, independently verified
integrator (Methods). The complete refined experiment required 1,436 MAP calls and 693.1 seconds of
core runtime, with successful termination throughout.
    Ensemble designs had lower exploratory mean-MSE estimates than nominal designs in several
extension settings, but the direction depended on objective and budget (Fig. 5). Of the eight prescribed
settings, four had 95% intervals that excluded zero, and all four favoured the ensemble: the shared
objective with all sites and M=5 (−85.51%, −98.81% to −8.92%), the single-response objective with
endpoints excluded and M=5 (−15.38%, −34.66% to −2.00%), and the shared objective with endpoints
excluded at M=5 (−13.56%, −28.56% to −4.78%) and M=10 (−22.84%, −49.24% to −5.33%). No
interval excluded zero in the opposite direction, but the single-response point estimate reversed at
M=10 with endpoints excluded (+9.45%, −9.56% to 23.28%). With only eight independent networks,
unadjusted pointwise intervals, and simultaneous changes in model family, dynamics and parameter
coordinates, the extension describes conditional behaviour and motivates a confirmatory study; it is
neither an N-only scaling effect nor a replacement for the primary study.

2.5 Approximation diagnostics constrain the interpretation
Development-only diagnostics measured local Taylor remainders and selection overlap. Mean calibration
remainders relative to parameter-induced activity changes were approximately 0.179 under matched
uncertainty, 0.334 under broad uncertainty and 0.252 under hidden bias shift. Broader uncertainty
changes signal scale and parameter variability as well as nonlinear behaviour, so these differences do not
isolate a curvature effect. Local and ensemble sets frequently overlapped. When sets were identical, the
fitted data and response predictions were identical by construction, limiting any decision benefit of a
changed risk value in those cases.

    The matched and hidden-shift conditions used the same public prior and therefore produced exactly
the same prospective designs, despite different true responses. This is an information boundary: without
a preliminary measurement, the selector cannot infer an undisclosed physiological shift. In the first frozen
evaluation, approximate 95% local MAP response intervals for the nominal scalar estimator covered the
truth with proportions 0.930, 0.919 and 0.793 for matched, broad and shifted conditions, respectively,
at excluded endpoints and M=3. These diagnostics identify limitations of the current information and
approximation contract; they do not establish a single biological mechanism for the observed errors.

3 Discussion
Prior-averaged local risk usually selects the same recordings as the nominal risk, so in those designs
it cannot change the prediction. In the remaining designs, neither a lower response error nor a more
accurate forecast than the nominal criterion is established. The three-step audit therefore shows cheaply
whether a costlier criterion is worth adopting.
    This study separates the value of a risk estimate from the value of the decision made with it. In the
primary model, prior-averaged local risk usually led to the same recordings, and where it did not, it did
not establish lower response error for either prediction objective. The separately specified forecast study
also left its primary improvement uncertain. The larger model family produced favourable effects in four
of eight exploratory settings and a reversal in another. Together, these results argue for evaluating a
recording criterion through the complete inference task, including the estimator and feasible observation
pool, rather than assuming that a broader risk approximation necessarily improves the final prediction.
They also suggest a practical check, supported here only for a 12-unit model under the primary condition:
before paying for a more elaborate criterion, measure how often it changes the design.
    A non-significant interval is more informative when read as an approximate bound than as a verdict
[26, 27]. For the shared three-response objective, the 97.5% interval (−2.95% to +11.51%) does not
extend below a mean-MSE reduction of about 3%, while it does extend to a deterioration of about 12%.
For the single-response objective the interval (−18.48% to +12.12%) is too wide to exclude either a
substantial gain or a substantial loss, and that comparison is where additional independent networks
would be most informative. These readings are approximate: 13 and 26 of the 40 network differences
are exactly zero and the rest are heavy-tailed, so percentile bootstrap intervals are not sharp, and no
equivalence margin was specified in advance.
    This comparison builds on connectome-constrained inference and goal-oriented design. Partial record-
ings and recording-site optimisation are already central to the former [9], while valuing observations
for a specified functional is established in the latter [20]. The factorial comparison keeps the response
objective fixed when assessing ensemble risk, so the familiar advantage of task specialisation cannot by
itself explain that contrast. The response test also follows the purpose-matched validation principle illus-
trated by neural perturbation studies [15]. The present evidence concerns this particular computational
comparison; it does not introduce a new optimal-design principle.
    Local risk may differ from actual response error for three reasons. First, the criterion averages delta-
method risks over a prior ensemble, whereas the evaluated predictor simulates q at a MAP parameter.
Second, it does not average nonlinear posteriors over future data. Third, it does not use the posterior
mean of q, the Bayes predictor for squared loss. Prediction-profile and profile-likelihood approaches
in dynamic models assess uncertainty in the predicted quantity or in identifiability directly, offering a
distinct alternative to local parameter approximations [28, 29]. The exact linear-Gaussian control links
analytic risk and MAP error under assumptions where those distinctions disappear. In the nonlinear
models, measured approximation remainders and overlapping selected sets are concrete diagnostics but
do not identify a single cause of the uncertain ensemble benefit. Small models with accurately integrated
posteriors would offer a useful next test of approximation error versus nonlinear plug-in prediction error.

3.1 Limitations
The decision-agreement result and the title rest on one condition: broad uncertainty, endpoints excluded,
three recordings and 12 units. Agreement for six recordings, other candidate pools and the 100-unit
family is left to future work. The objective contrast also changes the number of targets, and the factorial
interaction was not estimated, so task specialisation is separated from uncertainty handling only in
design, not in a tested interaction.

    The study assumes known numerical J, a correct observation equation, known initial conditions and
a specified stimulation action. Experimental-design studies in systems biology show that informative
experiments can expose model discrepancy [30], and weakly constrained parameter directions are
common in biological models [8]; structural misspecification is left to future work. There is no process
noise, plasticity or biological nonstationarity. Forty independent networks quantify uncertainty for the
prescribed generating distribution, not for biological networks in general. Finite ensembles, greedy
selection and common-start MAP introduce additional approximation and optimisation limits; successful
solver termination does not establish global optimality. The 100-unit extension remains synthetic and has
only eight independent networks. Jointly mapped neural recordings and perturbations would be needed
to assess external validity. Connecting the response functional to mechanical output would require a
further neuromuscular validation layer, as illustrated by current biohybrid crawlers [2, 4].
    No criterion was uniformly better than the others, so each should be tested on its own task, checking
both its forecasts and the recordings it chooses. The comparison sharpens the next question: which part
of the risk-to-prediction chain needs a better approximation, and can improving that part change the
observations enough to improve the response estimate? The 100-unit extension suggests where to look
first: the shared objective, and the endpoints-excluded pool at both budgets tried (M=5 and M=10
each had a shared-objective interval excluding zero). A confirmatory study would fix those settings, the
primary comparison and the number of independent networks before any outcome is inspected, and
would not reuse the present networks.

3.2 Implications for recording-design practice
Box 2 | A practical check before adopting a costlier criterion. (1) Compute both criteria on
your design pool. (2) Count the designs with identical recording sets. (3) If agreement is high, keep the
cheaper criterion unless a separate reason favours the other. (4) If agreement is low, run the error and
forecast checks of Box 1 on new networks and report the intervals as approximate bounds.

3.3 Scaling and next studies
Scaling in independent networks. The primary comparisons are limited by the number of independent
networks, not by computation: the 40-network evaluation took 356 s of core runtime. Planning calculations
from the frozen paired differences (a planning aid, not an inference; see Supplementary Note on planning)
give the number of new networks a confirmatory study would need for a chosen interval width; they are
reported with the caveat that heavy-tailed differences make normal approximations optimistic. With
the observed spread, a 97.5% interval whose half-width is 10%, 5% or 2.5% of the nominal mean MSE
would need about 80, 320 and more than 640 networks for the single-response comparison (normal
approximation: 82, 326 and 1,301), and about 40, 80 and 320 for the shared comparison (normal


                                                        10
approximation: 14, 56 and 223). The single-response figures are dominated by one network that supplies
66% of the summed absolute differences; without it the normal-approximation requirement falls to 11,
44 and 176. The forecast score has no natural scale, so for absolute half-widths of 0.4, 0.2 and 0.1 it
would need roughly 160, 640 and more than 640 networks (bootstrap). Because 13 of 40 and 26 of 40
network differences are exactly zero and the rest are heavy-tailed, these figures are optimistic and should
be read as orders of magnitude (Supplementary planning note).
    Scaling in model size. The exploratory 100-unit family cost about 0.48 s per MAP call (693.1 s for
1,436 calls) against about 0.009 s per call at 12 units (356.0 s for 38,610 calls), a roughly fifty-fold increase
per fit for an eight-fold increase in units, even with an exact low-rank state reduction. Extrapolating
from the measured per-network cost (about 87 s per 100-unit network against 9 s per 12-unit network in
the first frozen evaluation), 160 and 640 100-unit networks would take roughly 3.9 and 15.4 hours on the
study machine. Extending to larger connectome-scale models will therefore need cheaper sensitivities
(adjoint or low-rank updates) rather than more of the same compute.
    A confirmatory hypothesis. The exploratory extension points to one prespecified test: in the 100-
unit rank-two family with endpoints excluded, prior-averaged local risk lowers mean squared response
error for the shared three-response objective at M=5 and M=10. Two cautions apply. These settings
were chosen after seeing the eight-network results, so the observed effects are likely to be overestimated,
as selecting the most favourable settings after seeing the results typically does, and the planning figures
above come from the 12-unit study and are precision-based, not a power calculation for this 100-unit
effect; a pilot variance from new 100-unit networks would be needed first. A confirmatory study would
then fix this primary comparison, the number of independent networks, disjoint network seeds, the
paired network-cluster bootstrap and the decision rule before any outcome is inspected, and would
record design agreement alongside error so that a null result remains interpretable.
    Separating the sources of mismatch. The three possible reasons local risk can differ from actual
error (delta-method approximation, plug-in prediction at the MAP point, and the effect of design
selection) are separable in small models whose posteriors can be integrated accurately. Doing so would
show which part of the risk-to-prediction chain deserves a better approximation, and would test posterior-
based criteria, such as nonlinear expected-information designs, against the local ones under the same
audit.
    Toward biological evidence. External validity needs jointly mapped neural recordings and
perturbations with a one-to-one unit correspondence, the data design emphasised in recent perturbation-
based model evaluations [15]. Misspecified observation models, process noise, and a cost model for
recording sites would turn the present controlled setting into a design tool; connecting the response
functional to mechanical output in a biohybrid robot [1, 2] would require a further neuromuscular
validation layer.

4 Conclusions
Prior-averaged local risk usually leaves the recording decision unchanged in the simulated networks
studied here, and it did not establish a stable improvement in response error or in the accuracy of the
risk forecast. A larger exploratory family suggests that benefits, where they exist, depend on objective
and budget. Our contribution is a three-step audit (decision agreement, response error, forecast accuracy)
with open data and a proposed confirmatory test; it is not a new design principle, and it has no biological
validation. For interface designers the practical result is a cheap check: count how often a costlier
channel-selection criterion changes the channels before adopting it. Whether this holds for living tissue
is untested.

5.4 Common inference and paired evaluation
After selection, all methods minimise the same Gaussian MAP objective, using analytic sensitivities and
the prior mean as the initial parameter. The solver runs at most 80 function evaluations with identical
tolerances. A finite final iterate is retained and flagged if convergence is not reached. Exceptions or
nonfinite predictions trigger a flagged prior-mean fallback. No instance is silently dropped.
     Within each network, true parameter vector and noise repetition, methods receive their selected
subsets of one common measurement-noise field. The full field is internal to the generator. Identical
recorded datasets receive the same MAP objective, prior-mean starting point, common noise field, solver
settings and deterministic solver, so identical sets give identical fits and predictions by construction;
they may also reuse a cached fitted solution; this changes compute accounting, not information access.
Responses are predicted by nonlinear simulation at the fitted parameter. A passive input not used for
fitting gives an additional activity-prediction diagnostic.

5.6 Estimands and uncertainty
For each J, squared response errors are averaged across all prescribed truths, noises, targets and random-
design repetitions. Network risks are then averaged equally across J. RMSE is the square root of the
overall mean squared error. Repeated targets or noises do not create additional independent networks.
    Interval key: 97.5% intervals belong to the two prespecified first-study comparisons, the 95% interval
to the single prespecified forecast comparison, and all other intervals are pointwise 95% and exploratory.


                                                        13
Two prespecified primary comparisons, ES−LS and EV−LV, concern broad uncertainty, excluded
endpoints and M=3. The paired network-cluster percentile bootstrap [31, 32] uses 10,000 resamples of
whole networks, so repeated targets and noises do not count as independent. Each primary comparison
receives a 97.5% interval as a two-comparison Bonferroni interval arrangement. Other strata, task-
specialisation comparisons and factorial interactions have exploratory pointwise 95% intervals. We do
not infer equivalence from an interval crossing zero [26, 27].
    The primary scale is raw response MSE. A secondary scale uses an independent common 64-draw
nonlinear prior-Monte-Carlo variance. It is not method-specific. Prescribed targets below a frozen
variance floor of 10−16 retain raw results; a complete standardised estimand is NA rather than silently
excluding them. Local MAP intervals are approximate diagnostics, not guaranteed confidence intervals.

5.7 Separately specified risk-forecast follow-up
The first study distinguishes selection gains from apparent risk-estimation differences only exploratorily.
A subsequent protocol therefore uses new network IDs 6000–6039 and fixes the recording policies to LS
and LV. The point predictions and selected data are identical for the two risk forecasts being compared.
The forecasts are the nominal and ensemble local risks evaluated at that same recording set.
   The risk-forecast score is

                              S (v, e) = log v + e2 /v,    v = max(R̂, 10−24 ).                            (5)
                                                                        2
    For positive v, its conditional expectation is minimised at v=E[e ]. This makes it a consistent score
for a positive MSE forecast; it does not establish a full predictive distribution or require the errors to be
Gaussian. The log term penalises indiscriminate inflation. This is a use of established scoring principles
[33, 34], not a new score. The forecast-evaluation literature distinguishes calibration from sharpness
[35] and treats calibration as separate from point-prediction accuracy [36]; the nominal-versus-ensemble
comparison here mixes both, applies to a mean-squared-error forecast rather than a full predictive
distribution, and does not test calibration separately.
    The follow-up has one frozen primary comparison: ensemble minus nominal risk-forecast score for the
LS recording policy under broad uncertainty, excluded endpoints and M=3. It uses a paired network-
cluster 95% interval. Matched/shift conditions and LV are secondary. These results do not redefine the
first study’s primary hypotheses.

5.8 Numerical and published-model checks
The implementation is checked against an independent NumPy model, analytic finite differences,
nonordered observation subsets, a linear bias-cancellation null, and exact small-subset enumeration. A
separate nondegenerate linear-Gaussian positive control uses constant observation/target Jacobians,
independent prior truths and noise, and exact linear MAP predictions. It checks both nominal–ensemble
equivalence and the link between aT Ca and actual MSE. The original SVD criterion is independently
calibrated in its noiseless linear internal-state setting against Monte Carlo reconstruction, rather than
mislabelled as a noisy-rate baseline reproduction.
    The N=100 model-family extension uses the published rank-two tanh distribution, raw signed gains
g~N(1,0.92 ), zero bias and τ =0.5 [9]. Its inputs, known initial state, measurement noise and inference
are explicit adaptations. Negative gains are retained as mathematical parameters. The coarse Euler
implementation did not pass continuous-time convergence checks. The refined implementation uses
x=v+Lz, analytic filtering of the known held input, and RK4 on the reduced state and sensitivities.
Responses and their complete gradients are integrated at RK4 stages. It is compared with dense RK4
and finer substeps before execution. Eight new networks, two truths, two noise repetitions, three targets,
budgets 5/10 and both pools are used. The prior ensemble has 32 draws and MAP permits 200 evaluations.
These settings are frozen before formal extension outcomes, following numerical and timing checks. All
extension comparisons are exploratory. This extension changes both model family and N, so it is not a
controlled estimate of an N-only scaling law.
