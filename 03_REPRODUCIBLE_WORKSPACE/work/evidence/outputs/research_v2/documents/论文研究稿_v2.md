# Predictive-risk estimation and recording decisions under constrained neural observation

**Computational research draft, 16 October 2026.** This manuscript separates development, the first frozen evaluation, and an independently specified follow-up. All activity is simulated. It does not claim a new optimal-design principle, biological validation, or improved robot performance. The conference abstract is deferred until the evidence and article position have been reviewed.

## Introduction

A recording configuration should be evaluated against the purpose for which its measurements are collected. A neural model may be required to reconstruct activity over a population, or to estimate the response of a specified readout to a known stimulation protocol. These uses need not favour the same measurements. Under a limited recording budget, a useful design must connect its internal criterion to the prediction that will actually be made from the resulting observations.

The connection between neural parameters and function has a substantial history. Similar circuit activity can arise from different underlying parameter combinations [1]. More recent connectome-constrained models make the known and unknown components of inference explicit. Beiran and Litwin-Kumar fix connectivity while allowing neuronal properties to differ between a generating network and a fitted model; their work already includes partial recordings and the choice of recorded neurons [2]. Task-trained connectome-constrained models provide a complementary route from anatomical structure to response prediction, with experimental comparisons and uncertainty across compatible models [3]. Neither incomplete recordings nor the selection of informative neurons is an unexplored idea.

The validation task must also match the intended use. Sourmpis and colleagues assess neural models using perturbation outcomes, beyond agreement with unperturbed activity [4]. We inherit that evaluation principle rather than claim it as a discovery. Our design variable is the recording set. The model family, connectivity, calibration input, target stimulation and recording budget are held fixed within each comparison.

Goal-oriented experimental design provides established criteria for selecting observations according to a quantity of interest [5]. A local approximation is inexpensive, but a broader parameter ensemble may evaluate different sensitivities. This raises two distinct questions: whether the ensemble better estimates future prediction risk, and whether using that estimate changes the recording decision enough to improve the resulting prediction. An improvement in one does not establish an improvement in the other. Both also differ from the familiar advantage of specialising a design for a known target rather than serving several targets with one configuration.

We investigate these distinctions through a controlled comparison of scalar and vector response objectives, each evaluated at a nominal parameter or averaged over a common prior ensemble. The first frozen evaluation uses 40 independent connectivity matrices, with physiological parameters, measurement noise and readouts nested within each network. It does not establish a reliable improvement from ensemble-based selection in the prespecified primary comparisons. Development diagnostics and risk comparisons motivate a separate follow-up on 40 new networks, evaluating risk forecasts while holding the recording policy and point prediction fixed. Its primary interval also crosses zero. An exploratory 100-unit model-family extension yields condition-dependent results. Numerical and model-family checks are kept distinct from biological evidence.

## Related work

### Partial observations and neural model constraints

Known connectivity does not determine every neuronal property or guarantee unique parameter recovery [2]. We adopt a fixed numerical J and uncertain physiological parameters; we do not infer J from activity. This is a model-conditional inference setting, more restrictive than an experimental connectome that supplies counts or structural priors. Our noisy finite-time risk baseline also differs from the original noiseless projection criterion. The latter is independently calibrated under its own assumptions before any comparison is interpreted.

Lappalainen and colleagues combine structure, dynamics and task optimisation, with comparisons to neural response measurements [3]. We do not reproduce that evidence level. The present models are synthetic. A second published mathematical family tests dependence on modelling assumptions; it does not turn generated responses into observed physiology.

### Perturbation prediction and experimental design

Choosing a recording site, choosing a stimulation input and choosing a model class are separate decisions. We vary the first. Optimal perturbation-input design has its own direct neuroscience literature [6], and nonlinear network observability provides another route to selecting sensors [7]. Structural observability, initial-state recovery and prediction of a functional response under uncertain gain/bias are related but different problems. Dynamic sensing in biological data likewise already has independent-replicate validation [8].

The paired response in our simulations is a consequence of the specified structural dynamical model. It is not an experimentally identified causal effect in an organism. Correct J, intervention action and observation equations are assumptions, not findings.

### Local predictive risk and its evaluation

Scalar and vector versions of the trace criterion are both goal-oriented A-optimal designs [5]. For a vector Q and q=cᵀQ, an isotropic distribution over unknown c relates average scalar variance to vector trace risk. Allowing a design to specialise after c is known introduces a classical optimisation advantage. That advantage is not the main hypothesis tested here.

Nonlinear goal-oriented information design already has more complete sampling approaches [9]. Prediction-profile methods can assess functional uncertainty without requiring unique physiological parameters [10]. Parameter uncertainty and prediction uncertainty are also long-standing systems-biology concerns [11]. We use a finite ensemble of local criteria as an inexpensive approximation, not as a substitute for these methods or an exact posterior risk. Risk-forecast evaluation follows established scoring-rule principles [12]; the score used in the follow-up is not proposed as new.

## Methods

### Model, measurements and response

The primary model is

\[
\dot x=-x+Jr+u,\qquad
r_i=\exp(\eta_i)\operatorname{softplus}(x_i+b_i).
\]

N=12, x(0)=0 and J is known. The independent synthetic J matrices have 30% sparse connectivity, zero diagonal, column-wise excitatory/inhibitory signs, and spectral norm 0.65. The uncertain vector is θ=(η,b). We observe rate r, not internal state x. Time and activity are dimensionless.

Two known calibration inputs apply sinusoidal probes to three channels. Euler integration uses a step of 0.05 over six time units. Sampling every fourth step yields 60 observations per recorded neuron across the two calibration trials. Measurement noise is independent Gaussian with standard deviation 0.05.

A separate fixed stimulus of amplitude 0.6 acts during time 1–2. Three readouts are selected from structurally reachable nodes using J before physiological parameters or measurement noise are generated. The target family is

\[
q_k(\theta)=\int_0^6[r_{j_k}(t;\theta,u_*)-r_{j_k}(t;\theta,0)]dt.
\]

The primary implementation evaluates this integral by the matching left-point sum. Q consists of these three responses. The vector criterion and primary raw-error evaluation use equal weights over the same target family. Scalar designs specialise for one known target; vector designs use one configuration for all three. No observations from separately selected scalar configurations are pooled.

Recording budgets are M=3 and M=6. The candidate pool either includes all neurons or excludes the stimulation site and all three readouts. The latter pool is shared by every target and method.

### A factorial comparison of existing criteria

In whitened parameter coordinates, the local information matrix for a recording set M at parameter particle ℓ is

\[
H_{M,\ell}=I+\sum_{i\in M} F_{i,\ell}^{T}F_{i,\ell},
\]

where F includes the observation derivatives, prior parameter scale and noise standardisation. With aₖ,ℓ denoting the whitened target gradient, the scalar criterion is

\[
R_{k,\ell}(M)=a_{k,\ell}^{T}H_{M,\ell}^{-1}a_{k,\ell}.
\]

The vector criterion averages these risks over the three readouts. Local designs evaluate at μ=0; ensemble designs average the individually solved risks over 16 common prior draws. Averaging information before solving would define a different criterion and is not used.

| Design | Predictive objective | Parameter representation |
|---|---|---|
| LS | One specified response | Nominal parameter |
| ES | One specified response | Common prior ensemble |
| LV | Three-response vector | Nominal parameter |
| EV | Three-response vector | Common prior ensemble |
| Activity | Full calibration activity | Nominal parameter |
| Random | No risk objective | Five feasible random sets |

All risk-based methods use the same greedy forward selection and deterministic tie convention. These are greedy-selected sets, not certified global optima. Random permutations give nested budgets. The design API receives only the public model, prior, inputs and task. It receives neither generating parameters nor measured evaluation outcomes. There is no uncounted all-neuron pretraining or preliminary dataset.

The ensemble criterion is a **prior average of local delta-method risk**. It does not integrate future measurements and nonlinear posteriors, establish a variance upper bound, or capture all posterior modes.

### Common inference and paired evaluation

After selection, all methods minimise the same Gaussian MAP objective, using analytic sensitivities and the prior mean as the initial parameter. The solver allows 80 function evaluations with identical tolerances. A finite final iterate is retained and flagged if convergence is not reached. Exceptions or nonfinite predictions trigger a flagged prior-mean fallback. No instance is silently dropped.

Within each network, true parameter vector and noise repetition, methods receive their selected subsets of one common measurement-noise field. The full field is internal to the generator. Identical recorded datasets may reuse an identical fitted solution; this changes compute accounting, not information access. Responses are predicted by nonlinear simulation at the fitted parameter. A passive input not used for fitting provides an additional activity-prediction diagnostic.

### Development and the first frozen evaluation

| Development | 8 | 2 | 2 | 3 | Matched, broad, bias shift, gain-only, bias-only |

Matched and broad conditions use correctly specified Gaussian priors and generating standard deviations 0.18 and 0.35 respectively. Bias shift retains the design prior at 0.18 while adding 0.5 to the hidden true bias mean. The method is not informed of this shift. Development gain-only and bias-only conditions fix the other parameter group. Increasing a prior scale changes parameter variability, signal scale and nonlinearity together; it is not an isolated curvature intervention.

Connectivity, truth, noise, design ensembles, task-scale Monte Carlo, random sets and analysis use separate seed namespaces. The first evaluation uses network IDs 5000–5039, disjoint from development. The execution protocol and computational source were locally frozen before outcomes were inspected. This is not external preregistration.

### Estimands and uncertainty

For each J, squared response errors are averaged across all prescribed truths, noises, targets and random-design repetitions. Network risks are then averaged equally across J. RMSE is the square root of the overall mean squared error. Repeated targets or noises do not create additional independent networks.

Two prespecified primary comparisons, ES−LS and EV−LV, concern broad uncertainty, excluded endpoints and M=3. Paired network-cluster percentile bootstrap uses 10,000 resamples. Each primary comparison receives a 97.5% interval as a two-comparison Bonferroni interval arrangement. Other strata, task-specialisation comparisons and factorial interactions have exploratory pointwise 95% intervals. We do not infer equivalence from an interval crossing zero.

The primary scale is raw response MSE. A secondary scale uses an independent common 64-draw nonlinear prior-Monte-Carlo variance. It is not method-specific. Prescribed targets below a frozen variance floor of 10⁻¹⁶ retain raw results; a complete standardised estimand is NA rather than silently excluding them. Local MAP intervals are approximate diagnostics, not guaranteed confidence intervals.

### Independently specified risk-forecast follow-up

The first study distinguishes selection gains from apparent risk-estimation differences only exploratorily. A subsequent protocol therefore uses new network IDs 6000–6039 and fixes the recording policies to LS and LV. The point predictions and selected data are identical for the two risk forecasts being compared. The forecasts are the nominal and ensemble local risks evaluated at that same recording set.

The risk-forecast score is

\[
S(v,e)=\log v+e^2/v,\qquad v=\max(\hat R,10^{-24}).
\]

For positive v, its conditional expectation is minimised at v=E[e²]. This makes it a consistent score for a positive MSE forecast; it does not establish a full predictive distribution or require the errors to be Gaussian. The log term penalises indiscriminate inflation. This is a use of established scoring principles [12], not a new score.

The follow-up has one frozen primary comparison: ensemble minus nominal risk-forecast score for the LS recording policy under broad uncertainty, excluded endpoints and M=3. It uses a paired network-cluster 95% interval. Matched/shift conditions and LV are secondary. These results do not redefine the first study's primary hypotheses.

### Numerical and published-model checks

The implementation is checked against an independent NumPy model, analytic finite differences, nonordered observation subsets, a linear bias-cancellation null, and exact small-subset enumeration. A separate nondegenerate linear-Gaussian positive control uses constant observation/target Jacobians, independent prior truths and noise, and exact linear MAP predictions. It checks both nominal–ensemble equivalence and the link between aᵀCa and actual MSE. The original SVD criterion is independently calibrated in its noiseless linear internal-state setting against Monte Carlo reconstruction, rather than mislabelled as a noisy-rate baseline reproduction.

The N=100 model-family extension uses the published rank-two tanh distribution, raw signed gains g~N(1,0.9²), zero bias and τ=0.5 [2]. Its inputs, known initial state, measurement noise and inference are explicit adaptations. Negative gains are retained as mathematical parameters. The coarse Euler implementation did not pass continuous-time convergence checks. The refined implementation uses x=v+Lz, analytic filtering of the known held input, and RK4 on the reduced state and sensitivities. Responses and their complete gradients are integrated at RK4 stages. It is compared with dense RK4 and finer substeps before execution. Eight new networks, two truths, two noise repetitions, three targets, budgets 5/10 and both pools are used. The prior ensemble has 32 draws and MAP permits 200 evaluations. These settings are frozen before formal extension outcomes, following numerical and timing checks. All extension comparisons are exploratory. This extension changes both model family and N, so it is not a controlled estimate of an N-only scaling law.

## Results

### Implementation and numerical checks

Primary rate and sensitivity outputs agree with the independent implementation within approximately 3.4×10⁻¹⁶. Directional trajectory and response derivative discrepancies are approximately 2.5×10⁻¹¹ and 9.5×10⁻⁹. The linear bias-only paired-response null has a range of approximately 2.2×10⁻¹⁷ and zero target gradient. A nonzero six-unit target's greedy set matches its enumerated optimum; this is a small algorithm audit, not proof of global optimality in the main model.

Across fixed numerical examples, primary-model step refinement gives response and sensitivity discrepancies of approximately 0.2%. The N=100 coarse Euler discrepancies are substantially larger and are not used as continuous-model validation. Reduced RK4 with two internal substeps gives response-gradient discrepancies below approximately 4.3×10⁻⁵ against an eight-substep reference in the checked examples. Independent dense RK4 and finite differences also agree. These are case-based numerical checks rather than a uniform convergence theorem.

The separate linear-Gaussian positive control contains 12 systems and 144 target/design evaluations, using 8,192 independent truth/noise draws per system. Nominal and ensemble designs agree in all 48 design pairs. Fixed-set analytic risks and paired actual MSE differences are exactly equal. Analytic aᵀCa and empirical MSE agree within the prespecified 5.5 Gaussian Monte Carlo standard-error threshold in all cells; the maximum discrepancy is 3.15 standard errors. Averaging identical particles along the main greedy path introduces at most 1.89×10⁻¹⁵ floating-point variation, recorded separately from the fixed-set identities.

The independent linear SVD reference contains 12 stable N=100, rank-30 networks, five budgets, 20 random sets per network and 2,048 independent bias draws. Analytic and empirical reconstruction risks agree within the specified Monte Carlo checks. At M=8, mean analytic risks are approximately 0.0855 for greedy selection and 0.1600 for random selection. This verifies the reference geometry under its assumptions; it is not a new scientific result or a reproduction of the original figure.

These point estimates do not turn every row into a new primary comparison. Specialisation effects are more visible when target endpoints can be measured, while local and ensemble decisions often overlap. The complete distributions, pointwise intervals and influential networks are retained in the analysis files.

At the primary LS recording policy, aggregate actual MSE divided by nominal forecast risk is approximately 1.615; using the ensemble forecast at the same selected set gives approximately 1.103. In the matched condition these ratios are approximately 1.151 and 1.051, and under bias shift approximately 4.329 and 3.952. These first-study ratios are exploratory; a ratio of aggregates is not per-network calibration or a full predictive score. They motivate, rather than replace, the independent forecast follow-up.

Approximate MAP 95% interval coverage in the excluded-endpoint, M=3 LS condition is 0.930, 0.919 and 0.793 for matched, broad and bias shift. The drop under an undisclosed prior shift is a limitation. Neither using a wider forecast nor selecting with prior ensembles repairs information absent from the common prior.

### Development diagnostics constrain the explanation

Development-only diagnostics retain all networks and separate raw from valid-target standardised remainders. Mean calibration Taylor-remainder norms relative to parameter-induced activity changes are approximately 0.179 for matched, 0.334 for broad, 0.252 for bias shift, 0.301 for gain-only and 0.125 for bias-only. These are direct approximation diagnostics; they do not isolate curvature from all changes in signal and uncertainty.

The same public prior produces exactly the same designs in matched and hidden-shift conditions, despite different response remainders. This is expected from the information contract. High local–ensemble overlap also limits how much a revised risk estimate can alter prediction through a different recording set. These facts offer a constrained explanation for weak selection gains, not proof of a unique biological mechanism.

### The independent follow-up does not establish a general risk-forecast gain

The follow-up contains 6,480 task-evaluation records from 40 new networks and 3,006 optimiser calls, completed in 17.5 seconds. The primary ensemble-minus-nominal score difference is −0.45565, with a 95% paired-cluster interval of [−1.15857, 0.02367]. Nineteen network means favour the ensemble and 21 favour the nominal forecast. Thus, the primary result does not establish reliable improvement. There are no failed fits, fallbacks or risk-floor clipping events.

Both forecasts assess exactly the same fitted predictions: their raw MSE is 1.1781×10⁻⁴. Mean ensemble risk is 1.617 times mean nominal risk; the mean of casewise ratios is 3.716. These are different summaries and neither alone establishes calibrated risk. The bias-shift LS secondary score difference is −1.3583, with a pointwise interval [−2.2143, −0.6345], but both mean risks remain below actual MSE in that condition. This secondary result neither overrides the primary finding nor demonstrates correction of an unobserved shift.

## Discussion

The first evaluation separates task specialisation from uncertainty representation. Both scalar and vector criteria already belong to goal-oriented design. Comparing a scalar ensemble against a nominal vector would conflate these factors; the factorial comparison avoids attributing a familiar specialisation gain to ensemble modelling.

The results also distinguish a useful risk estimate from a useful change in action. If two risk approximations select the same recording set, they produce identical fitted predictions in this experiment, regardless of their numerical risk values. A better risk forecast could still support reporting or planning, but that benefit requires a different evaluation from prediction improvement. The independent follow-up holds the selected data and point predictor fixed to examine that question; its primary interval remains inconclusive. We therefore do not promote the exploratory aggregate calibration pattern to a confirmed general benefit.

Prior mismatch is another boundary. The prospective designs have no measured preliminary data and therefore cannot learn an undisclosed change in physiology before selecting observations. A future sequential design could use a shared, counted pilot dataset, but that would require a new information and cost contract. We do not interpret a prior ensemble as protection against arbitrary shifts.

Several limitations constrain the article. J and the observation equation are known; there is no process noise, plasticity, biological nonstationarity or uncertain stimulation action. MAP is a common estimator, not a guarantee of posterior integration or global optimisation. The ensemble is finite and the local-risk formula omits nonlinear posterior geometry. Forty independent networks support inference only for the specified generating distribution. The published mathematical extension is still synthetic and changes multiple modelling features at once.

The present evidence supports a controlled computational evaluation and a sharply defined distinction between risk estimation and recording decisions. A new-method article would require additional, independently validated insight beyond these established principles. Evidence of limited or absent benefit is retained as a result rather than used to motivate unbounded method accumulation.
