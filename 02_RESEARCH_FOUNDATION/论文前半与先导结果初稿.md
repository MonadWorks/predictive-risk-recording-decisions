# When does activity-oriented sensing suffice for predicting a specified neural perturbation response?

**Working manuscript, 16 October 2026.** This document contains an original Introduction, related-work positioning, a precise problem statement, and methods for the completed synthetic pilot. Preliminary results are separated from the proposed main study. No conference abstract is included. Biological validation, methodological novelty, and suitability for a full research article remain unestablished.

## 1. Introduction

The usefulness of a neural recording depends on the question that it is intended to answer. A model may be required to reconstruct activity across a circuit, or to estimate the change in a particular readout following a specified stimulation protocol. Under a limited recording budget, these objectives can assign different values to the same observation. For a biological interface, the relevant question is therefore not only how much activity can be reconstructed, but whether the available measurements constrain the response required by its intended use.

This problem sits within a long history of studying the relationship between neural parameters and circuit function. Prinz, Bucher and Marder showed that similar circuit activity can arise from substantially different parameter configurations [1]. More recent work has made this issue concrete for connectome-constrained models. Beiran and Litwin-Kumar examined how activity recordings constrain networks whose connectivity is fixed but neuronal properties are uncertain [2]. Their study already addresses partial recordings and the selection of recorded neurons. Connectome-constrained task models provide another route to relating anatomical structure to neural responses, while retaining uncertainty across compatible models [3]. These advances motivate a narrower question about the purpose for which a recording configuration is selected.

The required validation also depends on that purpose. Sourmpis and colleagues evaluated neural models against perturbation outcomes rather than relying only on their agreement with unperturbed activity [4]. Their results make the general distinction between fitting observations and predicting interventions part of the existing literature, rather than a new claim of the present study. Our question concerns an upstream decision: when the model family, connectivity, stimulation protocol and recording budget are held fixed, does selecting observations for activity reconstruction also support the estimation of a specified perturbation response?

Goal-oriented experimental design supplies established tools for comparing these objectives [5]. It directs measurements towards a quantity of interest rather than towards the recovery of every uncertain parameter. Applying this principle to a neural model does not, by itself, constitute a new method. A useful study must instead determine whether differences between recording objectives survive fair comparisons, whether they improve predictions made by the nonlinear fitted model, and when constraints on accessible recording sites remove those differences. In particular, a comparison against a baseline that reconstructs the complete response vector is needed to distinguish a specific target from a broader but otherwise matched response task.

We formulate this comparison in a controlled setting with known signed connectivity, uncertain neuronal gains and biases, and a fixed stimulation–readout pair. The quantity of interest is the time-integrated change in one readout relative to a paired unstimulated trajectory. Recording sets are selected without access to the generating parameters or evaluation outcomes. We then fit the same nonlinear model to the selected measurements and score its response prediction. The completed pilot establishes that this workflow is inexpensive to execute, but also exposes important limits: apparent benefits depend on endpoint accessibility, risk weighting and influential network instances. The next study should explain these limits before introducing additional methods or making broader biological claims.

## 2. Related work and the remaining question

### 2.1 Neural inference with fixed structure and uncertain physiology

Structural constraints and physiological measurements contribute different information. In the setting studied here, the numerical connectivity matrix is treated as known, while gain and bias remain uncertain. This follows the identifiable scope of connectome-constrained teacher–student studies [2], rather than the broader task of inferring connectivity from activity. It also differs from training an entire task-performing model and subsequently masking its readout. Every fit in our comparison receives only the measurements allowed by its recording budget.

Lappalainen and colleagues combine anatomical constraints, simplified dynamics and task optimisation, with comparisons to biological response measurements [3]. That level of validation is not available in our pilot. We use independently generated synthetic networks and do not describe them as a fly circuit, an experimental connectome, or a validated biological digital twin. An extension to an empirical connectivity structure would test dependence on topology; it would not turn generated activity into measured physiology.

### 2.2 Perturbation prediction and recording design

Prediction under intervention is a distinct model-evaluation task [4]. However, choosing a stimulation input, choosing recording locations, and choosing a model class are different design decisions. We vary only recording locations. The stimulus waveform, its site, the readout and the evaluation window are fixed before evaluating a model. Work on optimal perturbation inputs [6] is therefore a close methodological neighbour, not an activity that we repeat under another name.

The present target is also narrower than a causal effect estimated from observational biological data. The stimulation has an explicitly defined action in a generative dynamical model, which supplies the reference response. This allows a controlled test of prediction under model assumptions. It does not identify causal effects in a real organism or resolve uncertainty about how an experimental intervention acts on tissue.

### 2.3 Goal-oriented design and practical prediction uncertainty

Goal-oriented design distinguishes uncertainty in a chosen output from uncertainty in the complete parameter vector [5]. Local sensitivity approximations make such comparisons inexpensive, but their accuracy depends on the nominal model and the extent of nonlinearity. Systems-biology studies have also shown that parameter uncertainty and predictive uncertainty need not coincide [7,8]. These sources argue against treating a local covariance as a certified uncertainty estimate.

Our potential contribution is consequently an assessment of sufficiency and failure in a defined neural sensing problem. The study should establish when a matched activity or response criterion is adequate, and whether available sensitivity information anticipates departures from that regime. It should not claim the invention of task-oriented sensing, the discovery of parameter degeneracy, or a general failure of activity reconstruction.

Both the scalar target criterion and the integrated-response-vector criterion are goal-oriented A-optimal criteria. If q=cᵀQ and the target direction is unknown with E[ccᵀ]=I/N, the expected scalar variance for a fixed recording set equals the vector-average trace risk. Allowing a design to specialise after c is known therefore has an established optimisation advantage. The research question must go beyond that advantage. Existing nonlinear goal-oriented information designs [9], nonlinear sensor-selection methods [10], and biological dynamic-sensing studies [11] further delimit this space. Prediction-profile methods already distinguish uncertainty in a functional prediction from parameter uniqueness [12]. These approaches provide alternatives to examine when the local approximation used here fails; they are not additional components to combine without need.

## 3. Problem formulation

Let x(t) denote the internal state of an N-unit rate model, with activity r(t):

\[
\dot x(t)=-x(t)+Jr(t)+u(t),\qquad
r_i(t)=\exp(\eta_i)\operatorname{softplus}(x_i(t)+b_i).
\]

The connectivity J, initial state x(0)=0, input u and observation model are known. The uncertain parameters are θ=(η,b). Activity r, rather than the internal state x, is observed and reconstructed. All variables and time are dimensionless in the pilot.

For a recording set M and a fixed calibration protocol, measurements are

\[
y_{k,t,i}=r_i(t;\theta,u_k^{cal})+\epsilon_{k,t,i},\quad
i\in M,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\]

All designs share a candidate set, cardinality budget, sampling schedule and prior. The pilot uses a nominal prior mean θ₀=0. It does **not** estimate θ₀ from a shared preliminary dataset. No design receives unselected activities or the generating parameters.

The target quantity is

\[
q(\theta)=\Delta t\sum_t\left[r_j(t;\theta,u_*)-r_j(t;\theta,0)\right].
\]

Both trajectories use the same parameters and initial condition. The discretised expression matches the implemented left-point integration. The readout j differs from the stimulation site. The stimulus and readout are drawn before θ is generated, with no outcome-dependent filtering. We compare candidate pools that either permit all recording sites or exclude both endpoints of this original task.

The primary evaluation concerns the nonlinear prediction error q(θ̂_M)−q(θ_true). The design criterion is an internal approximation and is not used as its own proof of success.

## 4. A controlled comparison of recording objectives

### 4.1 Common inference and local design model

For each candidate neuron, forward sensitivities give the derivative of its calibration activity with respect to θ. Under the Gaussian measurement model, the local information contributed by a recording set is F_MᵀR⁻¹F_M. Together with prior precision Λ, this defines

\[
C_M=(\Lambda+F_M^TR^{-1}F_M)^{-1}.
\]

This is a local linear-Gaussian approximation around θ₀, not an exact nonlinear posterior. A target gradient a=∇q(θ₀) gives the goal criterion aᵀC_Ma. For a vector-valued activity task z(θ), the criterion is the normalised trace of G C_M Gᵀ, where G=∂z/∂θ.

All criteria use the same greedy forward selection procedure. This controls the selection algorithm across methods, but does not establish globally optimal sets at a fixed budget. After selection, parameters are fitted by minimising

\[
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{k,t,i\in M}
\left[r_i(t;\theta,u_k^{cal})-y_{k,t,i}\right]^2
+\frac12\theta^T\Lambda\theta.
\]

The solver, initialisation, stopping tolerances and maximum function evaluations are identical. The final q prediction is obtained from a nonlinear simulation at θ̂_M, not from the linear design approximation.

### 4.2 Baselines and information controls

| Recording criterion | Quantity whose local risk is minimised | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Calibration activity | All-neuron activity during calibration | Broad reconstruction comparator |
| Matched-stimulus activity | All-neuron trajectories under the target stimulus and paired control | Controls input and time-window mismatch |
| Integrated response vector | Time-integrated stimulus-minus-control response of every neuron | Strong task-matched comparator; q is one coordinate |
| Specified response | q alone | Established goal-oriented criterion |
| Parameter recovery | Trace of parameter covariance | A-optimal comparator |
| Random | No criterion; uniformly drawn feasible set | Budget-matched reference |
| Alternate task | Response for a second stimulus–readout pair | Exploratory cross-task specificity control |

The alternate task changes both the stimulus site and readout. It is not a strict permutation of the readout under a fixed stimulus; the original code field `shuffled_goal` is retained only for correspondence with the frozen records. For the endpoint-exclusion pool, restrictions refer to the original task, so the alternate task does not have symmetric endpoint access. Cross-task comparisons in that pool require this qualification.

For a given network, all methods use subsets of the same measurement-noise field. Every selected dataset has the same number of observations. Generating parameters and evaluation responses are isolated from selection. A separate passive input is used to assess all-neuron activity prediction after fitting.

### 4.3 Interpreting agreement between criteria

Let B=GᵀG/N for the integrated-response-vector task. When a lies in the range of B, the standard inequality aaᵀ≼κB holds with κ=aᵀB†a. It follows that aᵀCa≤κtr(BC) for positive semidefinite C. This relation explains how a broad response criterion can bound a target criterion, and how the target's alignment affects the bound. We use it as a starting diagnostic, not as a new theorem or a guarantee about nonlinear fitted error.

A useful extension would test whether a diagnostic computed at the design stage predicts actual error on held-out networks and targets. If that connection fails as physiological uncertainty grows, small parameter ensembles may be warranted to examine local-approximation error. Their introduction should address an observed failure, with the same information and compute accounting as the local baseline.

### 4.4 Numerical and statistical checks

The pilot uses Euler integration with Δt=0.05 over six time units. Analytic forward sensitivities are checked against central finite differences. A linear control fixes the dynamics and allows only an unknown additive bias. Under paired stimulation and control trajectories, their difference is independent of that bias. The q gradient should therefore vanish in this particular null model; this is not a claim about every linear-Gaussian prediction task.

Networks, rather than individual fits or time points, are the independent units in the exploratory summary. We report raw response RMSE alongside a secondary metric weighted by each task's local prior response variance. These answer different questions. Bootstrap intervals in the supplied summary concern paired absolute-error differences, not RMSE differences, and are exploratory across multiple comparisons. All generated main-study instances are retained. Post hoc influence diagnostics assess sensitivity without redefining the primary sample.

## 5. Completed pilot: preliminary evidence only

Twelve synthetic networks were evaluated at two recording budgets, M=3 and M=6, under two candidate pools and seven criteria. This produced 336 fits in 8.02 seconds of core computation on an Apple M4 Max Mac. Solver termination was successful for every fit; global optimisation was not established.

| Candidate pool | M | Raw q RMSE: activity | Raw q RMSE: response vector | Raw q RMSE: goal | Raw q RMSE: random |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| All sites | 3 | 0.002669 | 0.002239 | 0.000259 | 0.002446 |
| All sites | 6 | 0.001344 | 0.000740 | 0.000261 | 0.001752 |
| Excluding task endpoints | 3 | 0.002689 | 0.002447 | 0.002014 | 0.002417 |
| Excluding task endpoints | 6 | 0.002288 | 0.002424 | 0.002422 | 0.002196 |

These aggregates do not establish a general advantage. In the all-sites, M=6 comparison, one instance accounts for approximately 97% of activity-design squared error and 98% of random-design squared error. A post hoc omission of that instance removes the apparent advantage over those two comparators; the instance remains in every primary result. With endpoints excluded and M=6, the goal design does not improve raw RMSE over activity, random or parameter designs, despite ranking first under the secondary variance-weighted metric. Thus task scale, endpoint access and influential instances matter to the interpretation.

The full trajectory sensitivity check gives a relative discrepancy of approximately 2.77×10⁻¹¹. The linear bias-only null has a q range of approximately 1.77×10⁻¹⁶ across sampled biases and a zero target gradient. Halving the integration step changes the true response by at most approximately 0.405% across the twelve teachers. This does not yet establish numerical stability of every fitted model or ranking. A small enumeration audit agrees with greedy selection in two non-degenerate six-unit examples; three additional examples have zero target gradients and provide no nontrivial optimality check.

## 6. What remains before a full article

The pilot uses one noise realisation and one original task per network, a correctly specified model, known J, and a prior matched to the generating distribution. The results are therefore a feasibility check under favourable assumptions. They do not demonstrate robust biological prediction or a new experimental-design method.

The smallest useful continuation is to fix a family of readouts under the same stimulus, add within-network measurement-noise repeats, and examine endpoint accessibility together with nominal-parameter mismatch. Gain-only and bias-only controls can test whether target uncertainty is mainly a direct readout effect or depends on changes in network operating point. The original recording-selection baseline must be aligned with its published definition before claiming a comparison against prior work. A further model or empirical connectivity structure should be added only after this comparison yields a reproducible boundary worth explaining.

A self-contained computational paper would require such a boundary or mechanism, supported by independent evaluations. If the findings reduce to the expected geometry of an existing goal-oriented criterion, the appropriate output is a transparent benchmark or technical report. The conference abstract should be written from the evidence ultimately obtained.

**Planned methodological extension, not yet implemented.** A factorial comparison can separate two questions: scalar versus vector predictive objectives, and a single nominal parameter versus a finite common parameter ensemble. The ensemble candidate averages local predictive variances over parameters drawn from the same available prior. Both scalar and vector criteria receive this treatment, and every selected dataset is subsequently fitted with the same MAP estimator. This controls target specialisation separately from uncertainty representation. The candidate is an approximation built from established ideas, not an exact nonlinear Bayesian risk or a claim of a new optimal-design principle. Its inclusion in the main study depends on an identified limitation and independent nonlinear prediction results.
